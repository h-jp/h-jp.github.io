Whenever I read Su Shi’s *Ding Feng Bo*, I am struck by its strength and generosity of spirit. The following is an interpretive English rendering for this essay:

> <span class="verse-line">Heed not the rain beating through the trees and leaves.</span>
> <span class="verse-line">Why not sing and whistle, and walk on at ease?</span>
> <span class="verse-line">With bamboo staff and straw sandals, I feel lighter than on horseback.</span>
> <span class="verse-line">What is there to fear? In a straw cloak, I’ll face life’s mist and rain.</span>
> <span class="verse-line">A sharp spring breeze shakes off the wine; I feel a chill.</span>
> <span class="verse-line">Yet the slanting sunlight on the hill comes out to greet me.</span>
> <span class="verse-line">Looking back along the path swept by wind and rain,</span>
> <span class="verse-line">I head home: neither storm nor sunshine holds me now.</span>

In a few lines, the poem opens up a remarkably expansive view of life. Rain, a bamboo staff, straw sandals, evening sunlight, and the journey home are ordinary things. In Su Shi’s writing, they also express a way of meeting the world. What moves me is both the beauty of the language and the perspective it makes possible.

Here, I use “cognitive growth” to mean learning to understand our experiences more fully, reflect on our judgments, and see beyond the immediate moment.

## 1. Equanimity means not letting the storm define us

“Heed not the rain beating through the trees and leaves” does not suggest that Su Shi has failed to hear the rain. The leaves are being struck, and the journey is difficult. His words suggest a choice about how to respond: the storm is real, but it need not set the rhythm of his inner life.

Under pressure, it is easy to lose that distinction. One failure can seem like proof that we are incapable; a difficult relationship can cast doubt on every effort; a setback can make the future appear closed. In such moments, we may mistake a part for the whole, a temporary condition for a permanent one, or a feeling for a complete account of what has happened.

A broader perspective gives us room to reconsider. A difficulty is part of life, not a final verdict on it. Emotions deserve attention, but they do not tell us everything about a situation. For me, the composure expressed in the poem begins with this ability to make distinctions without denying what we feel.

## 2. Clarity means seeing both the situation and ourselves

“Why not sing and whistle, and walk on at ease?” is moving because Su Shi speaks from within the storm. He is not offering easy advice from a sheltered place. The rain continues, yet he finds a way to keep walking at his own pace.

Our interpretations can sometimes add to the burden of an already difficult situation. Under pressure, we may imagine the worst possible consequences. When misunderstood, we may repeatedly speculate about what others think. When the outcome is uncertain, we may treat our fears as predictions. Recognizing these habits does not make the original difficulty disappear, but it can help us respond more deliberately.

A clear-headed person can feel distress while still examining a judgment or choosing a next step. Pain need not determine every action. Growing in understanding can help us move from reacting immediately to noticing our responses, considering the situation, and deciding how to act. We cannot always control what happens, but we can sometimes change how we meet it.

## 3. “Lighter than on horseback” expresses inner freedom

“With bamboo staff and straw sandals, I feel lighter than on horseback.” I read this lightness as freedom from some of the burdens we place on ourselves. The image is humble, yet the speaker feels at ease.

The insistence that we must win, be recognized, obtain immediate results, or prove that we were right can become exhausting. Effort itself is not the problem. The burden grows when every outcome becomes a judgment of our worth, leaving no room for delay, error, or an ordinary change of plan.

When reputation, position, and other people’s judgments dominate our sense of worth, a change in circumstances can feel like a loss of identity. Reflection can help us develop steadier criteria: what matters over time, what deserves our effort, and which disappointments we need not dwell on. Life offers more than an immediate ranking of success and failure.

Su Shi’s image does not ask us to abandon ambition. It invites us to consider what we can put down while continuing to walk. That is the kind of lightness I find in the poem.

## 4. Acceptance can leave room for action

“In a straw cloak, I’ll face life’s mist and rain” expresses a willingness to continue despite uncertainty. The Chinese phrase “任平生” suggests, in this reading, an acceptance of what life brings without giving up the ability to act.

Equanimity is sometimes confused with indifference: nothing matters, so there is no reason to try. But composure can coexist with serious effort. We can recognize that the world is imperfect and still care about our work; acknowledge uncertainty and still choose a direction; accept a setback and still look for a useful next step.

Developing a broader perspective may help us remain steadier through change. Good fortune need not define our whole identity, and a difficult period need not erase everything that came before it. Reflection gives us room to loosen the grip of immediate gains and losses on our judgment.

## 5. “Neither storm nor sunshine” suggests clarity of mind

The closing lines bring the poem’s movement into focus: “Looking back along the path swept by wind and rain, I head home: neither storm nor sunshine holds me now.” What first sounds like detachment can, with experience, suggest a deeper understanding.

Inside a storm, the storm can seem to fill the world. In sunlight, we may wish the brightness would last forever. Looking back, however, we can see both as parts of the same journey. This does not deny pain or pleasure; it allows neither to become the whole story.

The shift I find valuable is from being absorbed in one event to seeing it within a longer life. A failure remains a failure, but it need not define the person who experienced it. A success remains worth celebrating, but it need not become something we must preserve at any cost. That wider view leaves more room to live.

## 6. Growing in understanding can help us feel more grounded

We often think of cognitive growth as gaining knowledge, finding more information, or mastering methods. Those things matter. In the context of this poem, I am also interested in how we understand ourselves and others, question our interpretations, and find our bearings when circumstances are difficult.

A broader perspective does not guarantee an easy life or constant optimism. It may help us notice that a painful moment is not the whole of our experience, understand our reactions more patiently, and keep a sense of direction when the outcome is unclear.

Clarity need not mean coldness, and equanimity need not mean indifference. Both can support a more attentive response to life. Perhaps part of maturity is the gradual movement from “Why must this storm happen to me?” toward “Storms are part of life, and I can still choose how to walk.”

## Conclusion

What I admire in *Ding Feng Bo* is Su Shi’s ability to make room for difficulty without surrendering his humor, warmth, and composure. The poem allows rain and sunlight, discomfort and ease, to belong to the same journey.

Reading it, I see a possibility for my own life: to examine experience more carefully, keep immediate judgments in perspective, and continue with greater freedom. Reflection does not remove the storm. It can help us see more than the storm and find our next step within it.
