An important innovation can begin with a small observation rather than a complete plan. An unexpected result, a familiar phenomenon, or a question that others have set aside may prompt us to reconsider how something works. What matters is the connection we make: an ordinary observation can lead to a new explanation when we question an established framework and notice relationships we had overlooked.

We often call this a “flash of insight.” For researchers, it is useful to see insight in relation to the knowledge and experience that precede it. A new idea may bring scattered observations, questions, and concepts into a more coherent pattern. That moment can open a direction for innovation, but the idea still needs to be developed and tested.

## Beginning with a Moment

The familiar story of Archimedes in the bath offers an image of this process. In the story, observing displaced water suggests a way to measure an irregular object’s volume. As an illustration, it connects a persistent question with an everyday observation. It should not be treated as direct evidence of how a particular discovery occurred or how insight works in the brain.

Other accounts of scientific discovery include Kekulé’s recollection of a snake biting its tail and Poincaré’s description of mathematical ideas arriving after sustained work. Such retrospective accounts need to be read with care. Their value here is as reminders to attend to the preparation surrounding an apparent breakthrough, rather than to expect a sudden idea to replace patient inquiry.

For research practice, the lesson is straightforward: keep meaningful questions in view, build knowledge over time, and observe unexpected results closely. These habits give us material to think with and help us recognize a promising connection when it appears.

## The Work Behind an Insight

In psychology, “insight” is an important concept. Gestalt psychology suggests that human beings do not always solve complex problems through linear reasoning. Sometimes, after prolonged confusion, people suddenly change the way they represent a problem and immediately see a solution. In other words, “figuring it out” is not merely acquiring one more piece of information. It is reorganizing the relationships among existing information.

This also suggests a practical reason to alternate concentrated work with time away from a problem. Walking, resting, or returning after a break may make it easier to notice a connection that repeated attempts have obscured. A useful research habit is to record these ideas and test them when work resumes, without assuming that every spontaneous thought is a breakthrough.

Insight often emerges from sustained effort. It can seem like lightning, while the work that made it possible remains less visible. For researchers, its value lies in the new question or explanation it makes available and in whether that idea withstands further investigation.

## True Innovation Means Suddenly Seeing a New Structure

Innovation often requires us to look beyond the visible result and ask about the mechanisms behind it. In software development, for example, a failed test is a starting point for investigation. Does the failure reflect a misunderstood requirement, a flaw in the code, an abnormal runtime state, or a problem in the test itself? Asking these questions moves the investigation from observing a result to explaining it.

This is also why our research group focuses on cognitive intelligence and intelligent software engineering. A software system is not merely a collection of code. It carries human requirements, intentions, knowledge, and decision logic. Requirements analysis, code generation, automated debugging, and program repair may appear to be engineering problems, but at a deeper level, they all involve cognitive processes such as understanding, reasoning, memory, reflection, and decision-making.

In automated debugging, methods that rely solely on pass/fail feedback can miss information about how a failure develops. Runtime traces, variable states, and previous debugging attempts can provide additional evidence for diagnosis. A research question for us is how agents can use this evidence in a loop of trace analysis, cause investigation, repair generation, and reuse of lessons from earlier attempts. The value of such a design must be established through evaluation; it should not be assumed merely because it resembles human reasoning.

## Before Reform, We Must First Change How We See Problems

From a philosophical perspective, innovation is not a simple accumulation of existing knowledge, but a transformation in how we understand the world. Kant emphasized that human beings do not passively receive the world; rather, they organize experience through their own cognitive structures. Thomas Kuhn, in *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*, argued that scientific development is not always continuous and incremental. Major breakthroughs often appear as paradigm shifts. When an old paradigm can no longer explain new problems, humanity needs to build a new cognitive framework.

This is equally meaningful for today’s research, education, and organizational reform. In many cases, we do not lack knowledge; we lack the ability to reorganize knowledge. We do not lack tools; we lack the courage to redefine problems. Whether a research direction is truly meaningful depends not only on what model is used or what experiment is conducted, but also on whether it offers a new perspective, reveals a new mechanism, and forms a new explanatory framework.

The idea of path dependence is also useful when thinking about reform. An organization may continue to follow procedures that worked well in the past, even as its circumstances change. Familiar routines can support efficiency, but they can also make alternatives harder to consider. Reform therefore calls for examining the assumptions behind established practices, alongside the available technology, resources, and institutional constraints.

## The Stronger AI Becomes, the More Humans Must Preserve the Ability to Ask Questions

In the age of rapidly developing artificial intelligence, people may easily form an illusion: as long as models are powerful enough, data is abundant enough, and computing resources are sufficient, innovation will naturally occur. But this is not the case. Large language models can generate text, code, and plans quickly, but they cannot replace human value judgment, problem definition, and creative thinking. Models can help us obtain answers faster, but humans must decide which questions are worth answering.

This is also the significance of cognitive intelligence research. Future AI should not merely be a more efficient execution tool. It should become an intelligent partner that helps humans understand complex problems, organize knowledge structures, reason reflectively, and collaborate creatively. A truly valuable AI system should not only be able to “answer,” but also to “understand,” “reason,” “reflect,” and “improve.”

Therefore, the stronger AI becomes, the more important it becomes to keep asking meaningful questions. Someone who does not question the task or its assumptions may only repeat existing paths faster, even with powerful tools. A person who can continue to ask good questions may use intelligent tools to open new directions. In the future, what truly matters is not only the ability to use AI, but also the ability to expand cognition and explore creatively with AI.

## The Value of Education Lies in Protecting Sparks of Thought

In [*On Listening to Lectures* (48c)](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Moralia/De_auditu%2A.html), Plutarch uses the image of kindling a fire to emphasize independent thought. This offers a useful way to think about education: students need more than ready-made answers. They need opportunities to formulate questions, organize ideas, and explore what is not yet understood.

In mentoring students, I increasingly see research training as a way to develop judgment through real problems. Progress becomes visible when a student formulates a meaningful question, explains an unexpected experimental result, turns a vague idea into a workable plan, or draws reusable lessons from a failed attempt. These are moments in which students begin to think more independently.

Educational reform should not focus only on updating tools. It should focus more on the cognitive development of people. We should not train students merely to master a particular software tool, model, or programming language. We should cultivate their ability to discover, abstract, model, and solve problems. For a research group, the most important thing is not to make students mechanically complete tasks, but to help them develop insight, judgment, and genuine research interest through real problems.

## From Insight to System: Our Exploration of Cognitive Intelligence

For us, a “flash of insight” is more than a personal experience; it also offers a metaphor for our research. The cognitive intelligence we pursue aims to transform human mechanisms of understanding, reasoning, memory, reflection, collaboration, and decision-making into intelligent system methods that are computable, implementable, and verifiable.

In intelligent requirements engineering, we study how machines can understand human requirements, recognize intentions, identify missing information, and transform vague expressions into structured specifications. In code intelligence, we study how machines can generate programs from requirements, locate errors through runtime traces, and continuously repair code based on feedback. In multi-agent systems, we study how different agents can divide work, collaborate, share knowledge, and form group-level reasoning capabilities. Although these directions appear to belong to software engineering and artificial intelligence, they all point to a shared deeper question: how can systems acquire cognitive processes closer to those of humans?

This is why we emphasize the research philosophy of “intelligence as the core, software as the soul, hardware as the support, and applications as the foundation.” It connects intelligent methods with the software that implements them, the hardware that supports them, and the tasks through which they are tested. We aim to build systems that help people understand problems, reason with evidence, learn from errors, and make complex decisions.

## Closing Thoughts

A flash of insight is the spark of innovation and reform. A cognitive leap is the starting point of intellectual breakthrough. Continuous practice is the path through which inspiration becomes reality. A truly valuable idea may begin as a question, an association, or an immature judgment. But if it is taken seriously, continuously refined, and systematically verified, it may grow into a new research direction, a new engineering method, or even a new social practice.

The progress of human civilization is never a simple repetition of the past. It is the continuous opening of new possibilities through repeated cognitive breakthroughs. We should cherish every flash of insight, and we should also understand, cultivate, and amplify it with philosophical depth, psychological insight, neuroscientific inspiration, sociological vision, and engineering practice.

Because in many cases, what truly changes the future is not an answer already written down, but a question that is seriously raised in a certain moment.
