We often place a different life on the far side of some condition: once I have enough money, I will study what interests me; once an opportunity arrives, I will develop my abilities seriously; once someone notices me, I will begin work worth noticing. Such waiting sometimes reflects real constraints. Sometimes it quietly becomes a habit. Over time, we come to assume that change must happen outside us first, while our part is to wait.

Yet if we keep allowing today's circumstances to define tomorrow's possibilities, better conditions may arrive without our knowing what to do with them. Having more resources and being able to conceive of a more worthwhile life each require attention. This leads to a deliberately provocative thought:

**We used to say that poverty limits imagination. The truth, however, is the reverse: the poverty of your imagination leads to poverty in your life.**

I would first turn this statement toward myself. Using it to explain all poverty would be crude: family circumstances, illness, access to education, social conditions, and chance can profoundly affect a person's situation. People struggling to meet immediate needs deserve understanding. The statement's value lies in drawing attention to a direction of influence we can overlook: circumstances constrain imagination, and our judgments about what is possible also help shape the circumstances that follow.

Once we decide that “this is all I can be,” many choices disappear before we consider them. Without attempts, we gain fewer new experiences; without new experiences, our existing judgment seems increasingly reliable. As this cycle continues, it becomes easy to assume that paths we have never taken do not exist. Poverty of imagination can become a narrowing of life through a succession of apparently reasonable decisions to give up.

Imagination here means recognizing possibilities we have not yet used. When reflecting on a period of work, can we identify the abilities we developed as well as the time we spent? When a difficulty keeps returning, can we conceive of a solution worth trying? After a failure, can we distinguish the method that did not work from the parts of our effort that still have room to grow?

In the age of AI, this capacity takes on greater significance. Ideas have often remained unexplored because we did not know how to begin, lacked a particular skill, or could not afford the cost of trying. At least in some tasks, the distance between an idea and a first draft is now shortening. An experiment by Noy and Zhang found that AI assistance improved efficiency and output quality in the professional writing tasks they studied. Some parts of turning an intention into usable work can already receive effective support from these tools. [Original study](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adh2586)

**In the age of AI, imagination is becoming increasingly important.** As more people can use tools to express an idea clearly and turn it into something that can be discussed and tried, the problems we notice, the possibilities we propose, and the directions we choose deserve greater attention. A thought once confined to the mind may gain a way into the world. A path once dismissed with “I cannot do that” may deserve another look. Better means of putting ideas into practice give imagination more opportunities to have a real effect.

Consider someone who handles similar tasks every day. They know where problems arise and what regularly slows their colleagues down. If they understand this experience only as another day endured, little may remain beyond the fatigue. Suppose instead that they imagine turning a recurring source of mistakes into a clear guide, then ask AI to help organize it, draft explanations, and suggest situations they may have overlooked. Everyday experience can become the starting point for an improvement. Asking a few colleagues to try it, observing what remains confusing, and revising it gives the idea substance in the real world.

The same tool can consequently play different roles in a life. It can help us complete existing tasks more quickly, and it can help us attempt work we have never done before. The latter possibility calls for attention to difficulties that habit has obscured, a willingness to imagine a better approach, and an effort to test it. As a tool's capabilities become available to us, our understanding of the needs around us matters more to what those capabilities help create.

AI can also participate in imagining possibilities, offering candidate directions and suggesting new combinations. In an experiment on short-story writing, work produced with access to AI ideas was judged more creative, while the resulting stories were also more similar to one another. This particular setting reminds us that obtaining more suggestions and developing a richer range of expression each deserve attention. [Original study](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290)

If we repeatedly begin with similar questions and accept the first response, powerful tools may also help us repeat familiar thinking faster. Personal experience, professional knowledge, and observation of specific circumstances remain worth developing. They help us ask substantive questions, recognize omissions, connect insights across fields, and judge which ideas merit further effort. Imagination nourished by knowledge and reality has a stronger basis for finding a direction of its own.

This step guarantees no increase in income and does not automatically remove unfairness. It does, however, give us a more active part in shaping our lives. Work that previously seemed only to consume time may begin to leave something behind: an artifact that can be improved, a transferable skill, an opportunity to collaborate. An AI-generated draft still needs checking, and a proposed improvement still needs to be tried. Through this work, imagination has a chance to become something of value that others can experience.

For this reason, imagination needs more detail than a wish. Wanting a better life is a beginning. Being able to explain whose problem we hope to solve, what resources are available, and what we can do first connects that beginning to reality. A vision containing only scenes of success, with no process, cost, or room for revision, can let us enjoy the result in advance while indefinitely postponing the work.

There is also a less visible limitation: a future may look ambitious while its contents have been borrowed entirely from others. We pursue whatever they pursue and value whatever they possess. When AI can quickly supply a complete plan for these wishes, we may have an apparently clear route before asking why we want to take it. Our wishes multiply while our understanding of ourselves weakens. Even after acquiring something, we still await the next comparison to establish what it is worth.

When imagination serves only to reproduce a life someone else already has, the freedom it offers remains limited. A deeper use of imagination is to consider a life suited to our own commitments: what work deserves sustained effort, which relationships deserve care, what material security we need, and what time should be protected for health, family, and solitude. These questions have no universal answer. They nevertheless determine what our efforts are for.

**Looking inward begins here: as our tools grow more powerful, gradually reclaiming the judgment with which we shape our lives.**

It begins with honesty. We need to recognize strengths while acknowledging abilities we have yet to develop, understand real needs while admitting that some wishes come from fear of falling behind, and see how the past has affected us while accepting responsibility for choices still available. As we learn the ground we stand on, we become better able to judge which seeds to plant, what growth will take time, and which expectations need adjustment.

This honesty also needs kindness. Explaining every setback as evidence that we are inadequate can turn reflection into endless self-reproach. More useful questions are specific: what did this judgment overlook, where was preparation insufficient, which conditions remain beyond our control, and what could change next time? Responsibility attached to something actionable gives us a way forward. Responsibility expanded into a condemnation of the whole self can make action harder.

Looking inward also requires continued contact with the world. Reading introduces lives we have not lived. Conversation brings judgments beyond our own experience. Collaboration reveals the limits of individual ability. Practice shows which ideas can withstand reality. An inner life that keeps growing needs this nourishment. Learning from others, asking for help, and seeking reasonable support can all be active choices in the shaping of a life.

Imagination and action therefore need a path that can be traveled in both directions. First describe a need we have observed, then use AI to explore different approaches and question their conditions and possible omissions. Choose a small attempt whose cost is manageable, revise the idea through real feedback, and continue. A long-neglected interest might become a regular period of study each week. A vague difficulty might become a question clear enough to discuss. A hope to do something someday might become a first draft that can be shown and improved. Each time a tool helps us accomplish something, we can also seek to understand that work a little better, so that our abilities grow through its use.

Small attempts matter because they bring back information. At a manageable cost, we can discover whether an activity holds our interest, whether someone needs what we are making, and whether the approach needs revision. Failure then has a more specific meaning: the present method needs to change. A single outcome does not have to carry a verdict on an entire life. For someone with family responsibilities and limited resources, that scale of effort deserves particular care.

Over time, another cycle becomes possible. We allow ourselves to imagine a small difference and use action to gather some evidence. The evidence makes our understanding more concrete and gives the next attempt a firmer basis. Confidence gains a practical source. Through repeated contact with reality, we discover that we can learn and adjust, and that there is still something we can contribute to unfinished work.

The Chinese phrase *sheng sheng bu xi*—life continually bringing forth new life—captures this capacity for renewal. It allows for rest, for periods when effort has no visible result, and for choosing again when an old direction comes to an end. A tree does not flower in every season. While its roots can still draw nourishment, it retains the possibility of new growth. An abundant inner life likewise includes curiosity, judgment, the ability to learn, and a willingness to reconnect with life after disappointment.

Material improvement still matters. Basic security, fair compensation for work, and room to make choices are worth pursuing seriously. Inner growth helps us pursue them with a clearer sense of what deserves our effort, what we should decline, and what already gives life substance. What we possess can then better support our lives, without life itself remaining forever postponed until we have a little more.

Returning to the opening statement, I would keep it as a daily reminder: our present circumstances have a history, but they need not decide every tomorrow. As AI expands the capabilities available to us, possibilities we previously ruled out especially deserve reconsideration. Which activities remain constrained by real conditions, and which have we dismissed through judgments formed in the past? What we lack can become a goal, while the experience, time, relationships, and abilities we already possess deserve renewed attention. We may be unable to leave every difficulty behind at once. We can still begin where some change is possible and let life move again.

Looking inward means putting down roots in our own lives. Continuing to grow means letting those roots draw nourishment from the real world, bringing forth new understanding, action, and possibility. We can let AI help make our ideas more concrete while reading, observation, and practice continue to enrich their sources. More powerful tools give us greater reason to consider what we want to use them for. This is why I believe imagination matters increasingly in the age of AI: as more things become possible to achieve, we have greater reason to imagine carefully which futures deserve our efforts to create them.
