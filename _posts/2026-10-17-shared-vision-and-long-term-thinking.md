“Another empty promise.” It is an easy response when someone talks about the future, personal growth, or what a project might eventually achieve. In Chinese, the expression is *hua bing*, literally “drawing a pancake.” It offers a quick judgment: what has not yet materialized cannot be trusted. The person making the judgment can sound clearheaded. Yet the value of a future undertaking cannot be fully present at the outset. If results must exist before an effort deserves support, many things that require patient work will never have a chance to begin.

I would argue that drawing such a picture of the future can itself be a form of long-term thinking. By this, I mean explaining a future worth pursuing, showing where today's effort could lead, and committing to build it with others. The same Chinese expression also describes deceptive promises, so the distinction matters. We need to examine the actions and responsibilities behind a vision. Its concern with something yet to happen is not, by itself, a reason to dismiss it.

Learning a difficult skill, starting a research project, and building a team all begin without finished results to show. People need to understand why an undertaking matters before they can invest time in it while the rewards remain uncertain. A vision gives their separate efforts a direction. It also lets participants decide whether that direction fits their own aspirations. Explaining the future is part of making cooperation possible.

The person proposing a vision therefore takes on a specific responsibility: to explain what that future might look like, how it connects to the present, and what they intend to contribute. A good plan can be ambitious, but people should still be able to find a first step. Time alone will not turn an attractive distant prospect into reality without effort now.

Short-termism can take hold in precisely this gap. It gives excessive weight to what is immediately available and too little to value that requires waiting, learning, and working together. An activity that does not quickly produce a grade, income, or recognition is judged unworthy. Someone describing a direction for the next few years may be dismissed as unrealistic. Each individual decision can appear to avoid a loss, while the pattern repeatedly closes off opportunities that require sustained effort.

Imagine, for example, a new research team planning to spend two or three years developing methods, data, and tools around a problem. In the first few months, members may mostly read papers, reproduce experiments, and document unsuccessful attempts. There may be no striking result. Measured only by what can be added to this month's output, the work can look slow. Considered in relation to the research that follows, its purpose becomes clearer. The team leader needs to explain that connection, and members need to see how their own abilities can develop through the work.

Long-term value must nevertheless leave signs along the way. After several months, the team should be able to discuss whether its methods are stronger, its questions clearer, its tools more useful, and its members more capable of working independently. A distant goal can remain out of reach while present work remains open to assessment. Long-term thinking calls for patience and an account of what that patience is helping to build.

Criticism of short-termism must also take people's circumstances seriously. Some need to cover living expenses; some have family responsibilities; others face limits imposed by graduation, employment, or health. Attention to immediate needs does not make them incapable of foresight. A long-term undertaking worth joining should make room for those needs. Asking others to surrender basic security today in exchange for distant benefits is difficult to reconcile with responsibility for a shared future.

Selfishness raises a different question: whose future is being pictured? Short-termism concerns how far ahead a person looks. Selfishness concerns whose interests they consider. The two can occur together, but they are distinct. Someone can be patient and plan many years ahead while treating other people merely as instruments for personal goals. Saying that an undertaking is long term does not establish that it deserves everyone's commitment.

Within a collaboration, selfishness may mean taking immediate benefits while avoiding the cost of building something together. A person might use resources and tools that others have maintained for years while consistently declining to contribute their own work. They might expect opportunities from a team but repeatedly avoid shared responsibilities. If everyone waits to receive a finished slice before deciding whether to help, little that depends on joint effort can be created.

Selfishness can also belong to the person proposing the vision. Others may be asked to bear the experimentation, waiting, and extra work while the proposer reserves the credit, resources, and decision-making power. Shared ideals are invoked when effort is needed, but recognition is treated as a personal favor when it is time to distribute the rewards. Such an arrangement remains selfish even if it lasts for years. Those who speak about the future should be especially clear about the costs they will bear and how other people's contributions will be recognized.

Having interests of one's own is ordinary and legitimate. People can reasonably expect growth, income, recognition, and better opportunities from cooperation. The difficulty arises when someone recognizes only their own claims and treats everyone else's as petty calculation. A credible vision makes room for these expectations and connects personal development to the shared undertaking. Cooperation has a stronger foundation when participants can explain its value to themselves and understand why it also calls for effort on behalf of others.

In the research team example, a leader asking for sustained commitment should provide regular discussion and feedback, arrange necessary resources, recognize contributions fairly, and support members as they become independent. Members, in turn, need to advance the work, communicate difficulties, and fulfill agreed responsibilities. These actions give people observable reasons to believe that things can improve. A relationship becomes difficult to sustain when one side must keep contributing while the other retains the authority to interpret every outcome on its own terms.

The practical question, then, is whether the proposed future can become action for which people take responsibility together. What comes first? Who will contribute? How will difficulties be handled? How will success be shared, and what happens to people's contributions if the effort falls short? A vision gains substance as these questions become easier to discuss. Someone willing to address them has stronger grounds for inviting others to commit their time to unfinished work.

Promises should also match what the person making them can reasonably take responsibility for. Research findings and project success often remain uncertain. Discussion, feedback, resource commitments, and fair evaluation include responsibilities that can be fulfilled now. Acknowledging uncertainty while meeting those responsibilities offers a sounder basis for trust than presenting success as inevitable. Pay that is due and support already agreed upon cannot be repeatedly postponed in the name of a distant vision.

Trust develops through these everyday actions. A carefully prepared discussion, support provided as agreed, and a fair acknowledgment of someone's contribution each help people decide whether a future remains worth working toward. The vision offers direction; daily conduct supplies reasons to believe in it. One useful way to judge a person's commitment to the long term is to observe whether they continue doing this work when there is little applause or immediate advantage.

Room for questions and revision is part of taking the future seriously. Participants should be able to ask about progress, discuss how rewards are shared, and reconsider their choices as circumstances change. Dismissing those questions as narrow-mindedness, disloyalty, or unwillingness to work makes a vision less credible. A shared future depends on continuing agreement. It cannot be sustained by demanding that people stop exercising judgment.

Patience also belongs alongside correction. An initial plan may prove unworkable, and further investment may only increase the loss. Reducing the scope, changing the method, or ending a collaboration can follow from careful attention to long-term consequences. An honest attempt that fails does not establish that the original vision was deceptive. Concealing the fact that a promise can no longer be fulfilled while continuing to demand effort from others carries a different responsibility.

We need to recognize empty promises without turning vigilance against deception into contempt for every possible future. If we trust only completed work, join only institutions others have already built, and accept only immediate returns, we can confine ourselves to the choices already available. Imagining a better future, explaining why it matters, and inviting others to help create it is an ability worth valuing.

The kind of vision I support takes practical form: the person proposing it commits effort of their own, makes a possible path visible, and ensures that participants have room to grow and benefit from what they help build. Work is already being done while the hoped-for result remains unfinished. As progress comes, those who contributed can share in it. Such a vision connects today's work to a longer view of life and deserves to be taken seriously.
